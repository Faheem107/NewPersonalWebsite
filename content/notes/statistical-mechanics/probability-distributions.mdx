There is something faintly absurd about statistical mechanics working at all. A litre
of gas contains something like $2.5 \times 10^{22}$ molecules. We cannot write down
their positions, we cannot measure their velocities, and if we could solve the
resulting $10^{23}$ coupled equations the answer would be useless. And yet the pressure
that gas exerts on its container is so reliable you can build an engine on it.

The resolution is that the difficulty inverts. Three bodies interacting
gravitationally is a famously unsolved problem. Ten to the twenty-three bodies
bouncing off each other is straightforward, because we stop asking about the bodies
and start asking about the distribution.

## Microstates and macrostates

<Definition title="Microstate">
A complete specification of the system: every position and every momentum. For $N$
classical particles in three dimensions that is a single point in a $6N$-dimensional
**phase space**.
</Definition>

<Definition title="Macrostate">
A specification in terms of the few quantities we actually measure — energy, volume,
particle number. Enormously many microstates correspond to the same macrostate.
</Definition>

The whole subject turns on that last sentence. Write $\Omega(E)$ for the number of
microstates consistent with a macrostate of energy $E$. The **fundamental postulate**
of statistical mechanics is that an isolated system in equilibrium is equally likely
to be found in any of them:

$$
P(\text{microstate } i) = \frac{1}{\Omega(E)}
$$

This is not derived. It is the assumption that does all the work, and it is justified
after the fact by the fact that everything built on it turns out to be true.

## Why the average becomes the certainty

The reason large numbers help is that the distribution of a sum sharpens as you add
terms. For $N$ independent contributions, the mean grows like $N$ and the standard
deviation like $\sqrt{N}$, so the *relative* fluctuation goes as

$$
\frac{\sigma}{\langle X \rangle} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
$$

<Aside>
For $N \sim 10^{23}$ this is about $3 \times 10^{-12}$. You would have to measure the
pressure in a room to twelve significant figures before you saw the gas being
statistical about it.
</Aside>

That single factor is why thermodynamics looks deterministic. The fluctuations have
not gone away — they are simply smaller than anything we can measure.

## The Boltzmann distribution

Now put our system in contact with a much larger reservoir at temperature $T$. The
combined system is isolated, so the fundamental postulate applies to it. Counting the
reservoir's microstates when our system holds energy $E_i$ and expanding the
reservoir's entropy to first order gives the single most useful formula in the
subject:

$$
P(E_i) = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}
$$

<Theorem title="Partition function">
The normalising constant

$$
Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}
$$

is not merely bookkeeping. Every thermodynamic quantity can be extracted from it by
differentiation — it encodes the entire equilibrium behaviour of the system.
</Theorem>

For instance the mean energy is a derivative of $\log Z$:

$$
\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial \log Z}{\partial \beta}
$$

which is worth checking by hand once, because it makes clear that $Z$ is a generating
function and the thermodynamics is just its moments.

## Three distributions, one derivation

Which distribution a system obeys depends only on what its particles are allowed to
do when they meet.

| | Occupancy | Distribution |
|---|---|---|
| Maxwell–Boltzmann | distinguishable, unlimited | $\bar{n} = e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}$ |
| Bose–Einstein | identical, unlimited | $\bar{n} = \dfrac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)} - 1}$ |
| Fermi–Dirac | identical, at most one | $\bar{n} = \dfrac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)} + 1}$ |

The three differ by a sign and a rule about double occupancy, and that is enough to
separate an ideal gas from a superfluid from the electrons in a metal.

stack upward and fill a Fermi sea. Bosons have no such restriction and collapse into
the ground state together.

## What to take away

Statistical mechanics does not predict what any particle will do. It predicts what
$10^{23}$ of them will do together, and the prediction is sharper than any measurement
we can make. The determinism of thermodynamics is a statistical illusion — but it is
an illusion accurate to twelve decimal places, which is a distinction without a
practical difference.
