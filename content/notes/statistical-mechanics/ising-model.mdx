Strip magnetism down until nothing is left but the essential and you get the Ising
model: spins $s_i = \pm 1$ on a lattice, each preferring to agree with its neighbours.

$$
H = -J \sum_{\langle i j \rangle} s_i s_j - h \sum_i s_i
$$

The first term is the interaction, summed over neighbouring pairs; the second is an
external field. That is the entire model, and it is still not fully solved in three
dimensions.

## The competition

Every phase transition is the same argument. The free energy is

$$
F = U - TS
$$

and the two terms want opposite things. Energy $U$ is minimised by order, with all spins
aligned. Entropy $S$ is maximised by disorder, with spins arranged every which way, of
which there are vastly more configurations. Temperature sets the exchange rate.

At low $T$ the energy term wins and the system magnetises. At high $T$ the entropy
term wins and it does not. Somewhere between, they trade places, and *that* is the
critical temperature.

## Dimension decides

<Statement kind="Result" title="One dimension: no transition">
Peierls' argument. In a chain, breaking the ordered state costs a fixed energy $2J$
for a single domain wall, but the wall can sit in any of $N$ places, giving it
entropy $k_B \ln N$. The free energy change is $2J - k_B T \ln N$, which is negative
for any $T > 0$ once $N$ is large. Order is destroyed at every finite temperature.
</Statement>

<Statement kind="Result" title="Two dimensions: Onsager">
In two dimensions a domain wall must be a closed loop, and its energy grows with its
length. Entropy no longer wins automatically. Onsager solved the model exactly in
1944 and found

$$
\frac{k_B T_c}{J} = \frac{2}{\ln(1 + \sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269
$$

It remains one of the very few exactly solved interacting models in physics.
</Statement>

The lesson generalises well beyond magnets: whether a system can order depends on the
dimensionality of the space it lives in, because dimensionality controls how much
entropy a defect can carry.

## Mean field theory, and its honest failure

Replace each spin's neighbours by their average and the model becomes self-consistent:

$$
m = \tanh\!\left(\frac{zJm + h}{k_B T}\right)
$$

with $z$ the coordination number. Expanding near $m = 0$ gives $T_c = zJ/k_B$ and
$m \sim (T_c - T)^{1/2}$.

The structure is right and the numbers are wrong. Mean field predicts a transition in
one dimension, where there is none. It gets $\beta = 1/2$ where the exact
two-dimensional answer is $1/8$. The approximation throws away fluctuations, and near
a critical point fluctuations are the entire story. Correlation lengths diverge and
the "average neighbour" stops meaning anything.

## Universality

The remarkable part is what happens when you measure the exponents. Near $T_c$,

$$
m \sim |t|^{\beta}, \qquad \xi \sim |t|^{-\nu}, \qquad C \sim |t|^{-\alpha}
$$

with $t = (T - T_c)/T_c$. These exponents do not depend on the lattice, or on $J$, or
on the microscopic details at all. They depend only on the dimension of space and the
symmetry of the order parameter.

A uniaxial ferromagnet, a liquid–gas critical point, and a binary alloy unmixing have
nothing physically in common and identical critical exponents. Explaining that
coincidence is what the renormalisation group is for, and it is the closest thing
statistical mechanics has to a miracle.
