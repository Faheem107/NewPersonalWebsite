---
title: The Partition Function
summary: One sum over states, and how every thermodynamic quantity falls out of differentiating it.

updated: July 2026
---

Put a system in contact with a large reservoir at temperature $T$ and ask how likely you
are to find it in a particular microstate of energy $E_i$. The answer, worked from
counting the reservoir's states, is the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\beta E_i}$ with $\beta =
1/k_B T$. Higher-energy states are less likely, and the temperature sets how steeply.

To turn a set of relative weights into probabilities you have to normalise them. The sum
that does the normalising turns out to carry, on its own, everything thermodynamics wants
to know about the system.

## One sum

$$
Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}, \qquad p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}.
$$

$Z$ is the partition function. The name is a translation of the German *Zustandssumme*,
sum over states, which says exactly what it is. It looks like a bookkeeping device, a
denominator you carry around to keep the probabilities adding to one.

It is much more than that, because the energies $E_i$ depend on the volume, the field,
the particle number, and $Z$ depends on temperature through $\beta$. Differentiating it
therefore reaches every thermodynamic quantity at once.

## Everything by differentiation

The mean energy is a derivative of $\ln Z$ in $\beta$:

$$
\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta}.
$$

The bridge to thermodynamics is a single further identification, the Helmholtz free
energy:

<Statement kind="Definition" title="The bridge equation">
$$
F = -k_B T \ln Z.
$$
Every equilibrium property follows by differentiating $F$: entropy as $-\partial F/\partial
T$, pressure as $-\partial F/\partial V$, and so on. The partition function is the point
where the microscopic sum meets the classical potential of the last set of notes.
</Statement>

The variance of the energy is a second derivative, and it is not just a curiosity:

$$
\langle E^2 \rangle - \langle E \rangle^2 = \frac{\partial^2 \ln Z}{\partial \beta^2}
= k_B T^2 C_V.
$$

The size of the energy fluctuations is fixed by the heat capacity. A system that stores
heat easily is one whose energy wanders, which is the fluctuation-dissipation idea in its
simplest dress.

## Independence makes it multiply

If a system splits into parts that do not interact, its energy is a sum and its partition
function is a product:

$$
Z = Z_1 Z_2 \cdots, \qquad \ln Z = \sum_k \ln Z_k.
$$

Because $F$ is a logarithm of $Z$, the free energies then simply add, which is what lets
you build a gas of $N$ non-interacting particles out of one-particle sums.

<Statement kind="Consequence" title="The two-level system">
For a single object with just two states, energies $0$ and $\varepsilon$, $Z = 1 +
e^{-\beta\varepsilon}$. The mean energy $\varepsilon/(e^{\beta\varepsilon}+1)$ rises from
zero at low temperature toward $\varepsilon/2$ at high, and its derivative, the heat
capacity, peaks at a temperature of order $\varepsilon/k_B$. That peak, the Schottky
anomaly, is a real signature of a gap in a material's spectrum.
</Statement>

So the recipe for a system in the canonical ensemble is short. Write down its energies,
sum the Boltzmann factors, take the log, and differentiate. The physics is in the first
step; the rest is calculus.
