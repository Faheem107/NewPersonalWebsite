Almost every partial differential equation solved by hand is solved the same way:
guess that the solution factorises, and see whether the equation permits it.

## The method

Take the heat equation on a bar of length $L$, held at zero at both ends:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}
$$

Try $u(x,t) = X(x)\,T(t)$. Substituting and dividing through by $XT$:

$$
\frac{1}{\alpha T}\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{1}{X}\frac{d^2X}{dx^2}
$$

The left side depends only on $t$, the right only on $x$. Two functions of independent
variables can agree everywhere only by both being constant. Call it $-\lambda$ and the
PDE becomes two ODEs.

<Aside>
The sign of the separation constant is not arbitrary. Choosing $+\lambda$ gives
growing exponentials in time, which the boundary conditions then exclude — the
physics rejects them, not the algebra.
</Aside>

## The boundary conditions do the real work

$X'' + \lambda X = 0$ with $X(0) = X(L) = 0$ has solutions only for

$$
\lambda_n = \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2, \qquad X_n = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}
$$

This is an eigenvalue problem, and the discreteness is imposed by the boundary, not by
the equation. A bounded domain quantises. That single observation is the entire origin
of quantised energy levels in quantum mechanics — the Schrödinger equation is a
boundary value problem and the integers come from the box.

Superposing and matching the initial condition:

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\, e^{-\alpha \lambda_n t}
$$

with $b_n$ the Fourier sine coefficients of $u(x,0)$.

## Why high modes vanish first

Each term decays at rate $\alpha\lambda_n \propto n^2$. Fine structure — large $n$ —
disappears quadratically faster than coarse structure.

This is why heat diffusion smooths, and why running it backwards is hopeless: the
inverse problem amplifies exactly the modes that carry the least reliable information.
It is the same reason deblurring an image is hard.

## When it fails

Separation requires the boundary to be a coordinate surface. A rectangle in Cartesians,
a disc in polars, a sphere in sphericals — fine. An L-shaped domain, or a
non-concentric annulus — no coordinate system separates it, and no amount of
cleverness will make it.

There are exactly eleven coordinate systems in which the Helmholtz equation separates.
Everything else needs conformal mapping, Green's functions, or a computer.
