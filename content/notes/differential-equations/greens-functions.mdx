Green's functions turn on a linearity argument so simple it is easy to miss how much
it buys.

## The idea

For a linear operator $\mathcal{L}$, define $G$ as the response to a point source:

$$
\mathcal{L}\, G(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') = \delta(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}')
$$

Then for *any* source $f$, the solution of $\mathcal{L}u = f$ is

$$
u(\mathbf{r}) = \int G(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')\, f(\mathbf{r}')\, d^3r'
$$

Decompose the source into points, add up the responses. The whole method is the
superposition principle taken seriously.

</Plot>

<Definition title="What G actually is">
$G$ is the inverse of $\mathcal{L}$, written as an integral kernel. Solving a
differential equation becomes doing an integral — and integration is a far better
conditioned operation than differentiation.
</Definition>

## The Laplacian

In three dimensions, $\nabla^2 G = \delta^3(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}')$ gives

$$
G = -\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|}
$$

and the solution of Poisson's equation is Coulomb's law integrated over the charge.
Which is to say Coulomb's law *is* the Green's function of the Laplacian, and always
was.

The $1/r$ is not a coincidence of electrostatics. It is geometry: flux spreads over a
sphere whose area grows as $r^2$. In two dimensions the same argument gives $\ln r$,
which is why line charges have logarithmic potentials.

## Boundaries

The free-space $G$ solves the problem only in infinite space. With boundaries, add any
solution of the homogeneous equation that cancels $G$ on the surface:

$$
G_D(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}') = -\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|} + H(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}')
$$

For a grounded plane, $H$ is precisely the image charge from electrostatics. The
method of images was a Green's function all along.

<Statement kind="Property" title="Reciprocity">
$G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}') = G(\mathbf{r}',\mathbf{r})$ for self-adjoint operators.
The response at $A$ to a source at $B$ equals the response at $B$ to the same source
at $A$ — true for sound in a room, for antennas, and for seismic waves.
</Statement>

## Causality

For time-dependent problems the wave operator has two Green's functions: retarded
(effect after cause) and advanced (effect before it). Both are mathematically valid;
the equations do not choose.

We impose causality by hand, discarding the advanced solution. That choice is not in
Maxwell's equations, and where it comes from is not a settled question — most
attempts to explain it end up pointing back at the second law.

## Perturbation theory

Write $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_0 + \varepsilon V$ where $G_0$ is known. Iterating
gives

$$
G = G_0 + G_0 V G_0 + G_0 V G_0 V G_0 + \cdots
$$

Every term is "propagate freely, scatter, propagate freely, scatter…". Drawing each
one gives you Feynman diagrams; the perturbative expansion of quantum field theory is
this series with a more elaborate $G_0$.
