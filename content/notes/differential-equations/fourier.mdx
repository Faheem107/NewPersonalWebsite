The Sturm-Liouville notes ended with a promise: a well-posed boundary value problem hands
you an orthogonal basis of eigenfunctions, and any reasonable function can be written in
it. The oldest and most useful instance of that promise is the one where the operator is
just the second derivative and the interval is a finite line. Its eigenfunctions are sines
and cosines, and the expansion is the Fourier series.

## The simplest eigenproblem

On $[-L, L]$ with periodic boundary conditions, the operator $-d^2/dx^2$ has eigenfunctions

$$
e^{i n\pi x / L}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z},
$$

and they are orthogonal, because $-d^2/dx^2$ is a Sturm-Liouville operator and its
eigenfunctions for distinct eigenvalues must be. Any periodic $f$ then expands as

$$
f(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, e^{i n \pi x / L}, \qquad
c_n = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\, e^{-i n \pi x / L}\,dx.
$$

The coefficient formula is just the projection onto one basis vector, the same inner
product that gives a component of an ordinary vector. Nothing here is special to sines; it
is orthogonality doing the work.

<Aside>
Convergence is where the subtlety lives. The series converges in the mean-square sense for
any square-integrable $f$, but at a jump it overshoots by a fixed fraction that never
shrinks, only narrows. That is the Gibbs phenomenon, and it is a property of the partial
sums, not a failure of the series.
</Aside>

## Energy is preserved

Orthonormality gives Parseval's identity, that the total size of a function equals the
total size of its coefficients:

$$
\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} |f(x)|^2\,dx = \sum_n |c_n|^2.
$$

Physically this is a conservation statement: the energy in a wave is the same whether you
count it in space or across its frequency components. Nothing is lost in changing basis.

## Letting the interval open up

The Fourier series is tied to a finite interval, which quantises the allowed frequencies
into the discrete set $n\pi/L$. Let $L \to \infty$ and those frequencies crowd together
into a continuum. The sum becomes an integral and the series becomes the Fourier
transform:

$$
\tilde{f}(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx, \qquad
f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \tilde{f}(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk.
$$

<Statement kind="Theorem" title="Why the transform tames differential equations">
Under the transform, $d/dx$ becomes multiplication by $ik$. A linear differential
equation with constant coefficients therefore turns into an algebraic one for
$\tilde{f}(k)$, which you solve by division and invert. Differentiation, the hard
operation, has been traded for multiplication, the easy one.
</Statement>

## The heat equation in one line

Take $\partial_t u = D\,\partial_x^2 u$ and transform in $x$. The spatial derivative
becomes $-k^2$, leaving $\partial_t \tilde{u} = -Dk^2\tilde{u}$, which is just
exponential decay:

$$
\tilde{u}(k,t) = \tilde{u}(k,0)\,e^{-Dk^2 t}.
$$

Every mode decays, and the sharp ones, large $k$, decay fastest. Inverting the transform
turns that back into a spreading Gaussian, which is why heat smooths a profile from its
roughest features first. The transform did not just solve the equation; it said plainly
which part of the initial data survives longest.
