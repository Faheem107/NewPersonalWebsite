A vector space is a set you can add and scale. That is nearly the whole definition, and
it is deliberately thin: anything satisfying the axioms inherits every theorem proved
from them, whether its elements are arrows, polynomials, or quantum states.

## Inner products give you geometry

Addition and scaling alone give no notion of length or angle. An inner product does:

$$
\langle u, v \rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{C}
$$

conjugate-symmetric, linear in one argument, and positive definite. From it,
$\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$, and orthogonality means $\langle u,v\rangle = 0$.

<Theorem title="Cauchy–Schwarz">
$$
|\langle u, v \rangle| \leq \|u\| \, \|v\|
$$

with equality precisely when $u$ and $v$ are parallel.
</Theorem>

This one inequality is the triangle inequality, the correlation coefficient's bound of
$\pm 1$, and, applied to position and momentum operators, the Heisenberg uncertainty
principle. Uncertainty is not a statement about disturbing what you measure; it is
Cauchy–Schwarz applied to non-commuting operators.

## Functions are vectors

Take continuous functions on $[a,b]$ with

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b \overline{f(x)}\, g(x)\, w(x)\, dx
$$

Every axiom holds. The space is infinite-dimensional, and the Sturm–Liouville
eigenfunctions from the previous subject are an orthogonal basis for it.

<Statement kind="Consequence" title="Fourier series, demystified">
Expanding a function in sines and cosines is not a special technique. It is resolving
a vector into components along an orthonormal basis:

$$
c_n = \frac{\langle e_n, f \rangle}{\langle e_n, e_n \rangle}
$$

the same formula as projecting an arrow onto an axis.
</Statement>

## Completeness

Finite-dimensional spaces are all the same up to isomorphism. Infinite-dimensional
ones are not, and the distinction that matters is whether Cauchy sequences converge
*inside* the space.

A **Hilbert space** is an inner product space that is complete. $L^2$, the square
integrable functions, is the canonical example, and it is the state space of quantum
mechanics. Completeness is what guarantees that an infinite superposition of states is
still a state, which is not a technicality you can skip.

## Gram–Schmidt

Any linearly independent set can be orthogonalised:

$$
u_k = v_k - \sum_{j<k} \frac{\langle u_j, v_k \rangle}{\langle u_j, u_j \rangle} u_j
$$

Run it on $\{1, x, x^2, \ldots\}$ over $[-1,1]$ and the Legendre polynomials come out.
Run it with weight $e^{-x^2}$ over the real line and you get Hermite polynomials. The
classical orthogonal polynomials are Gram–Schmidt applied to monomials under different
weights, nothing more exotic than that.

<Aside>
Classical Gram–Schmidt is numerically poor: rounding errors accumulate and the later
vectors drift out of orthogonality. Modified Gram–Schmidt, which subtracts each projection
as soon as it is computed, is algebraically identical and far more stable. In production
one usually reaches for a Householder QR instead.
</Aside>

## Dual spaces, and why physicists write bras

Every vector space has a dual $V^*$: the space of linear maps $V \to \mathbb{C}$. In
finite dimensions the two have the same dimension, and an inner product gives a canonical
correspondence between them.

That correspondence is exactly Dirac notation. The ket $|\psi\rangle$ is a vector; the bra
$\langle\psi|$ is the element of the dual space the inner product associates with it; and
$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ is that functional applied to that vector. The notation is not a
physicist's shorthand for something sloppier. It is the cleanest available way of writing
the pairing between a space and its dual.

<Theorem title="Riesz representation">
In a Hilbert space, every continuous linear functional $f$ is given by an inner product
with a unique vector $v_f$:

$$
f(x) = \langle v_f, x \rangle
$$
</Theorem>

This is what guarantees the bra–ket correspondence is well defined in infinite dimensions,
where "same dimension" is no longer an argument. It is also the reason observables can be
represented by operators at all.
