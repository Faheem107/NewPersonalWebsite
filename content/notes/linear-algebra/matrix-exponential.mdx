A single linear equation $\dot{x} = ax$ has the solution everyone knows, $x(t) = e^{at}
x(0)$. A coupled system of them,

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\,\mathbf{x},
$$

looks harder because the variables feed into each other. It is not, and the reason is that
the same exponential works if you are willing to exponentiate a matrix.

## Defining the exponential of a matrix

There is only one sensible definition, the power series, which converges for every square
matrix:

$$
e^{A} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{A^n}{n!} = I + A + \frac{A^2}{2!} + \cdots
$$

With it, $e^{At}$ has derivative $A e^{At}$, exactly as the scalar case does, so

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{At}\,\mathbf{x}(0)
$$

solves the system for any initial condition. The whole difficulty has moved into one
place: actually computing $e^{At}$.

## Computing it through the eigenbasis

Summing an infinite series of matrix powers by hand is hopeless. The eigenbasis makes it
trivial. If $A = P\Lambda P^{-1}$ is diagonalisable, then $A^n = P\Lambda^n P^{-1}$, and
the whole series collapses:

$$
e^{At} = P\,e^{\Lambda t}\,P^{-1}, \qquad
e^{\Lambda t} = \operatorname{diag}\!\big(e^{\lambda_1 t}, \dots, e^{\lambda_n t}\big).
$$

Exponentiating a diagonal matrix is just exponentiating its entries. So the recipe is:
find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, exponentiate the eigenvalues, transform back.

<Statement kind="Consequence" title="The eigenvalues run the dynamics">
In the eigenbasis the system uncouples into independent scalar equations $\dot{y}_i =
\lambda_i y_i$. Each eigenvalue is one decoupled mode: a negative real part decays, a
positive one grows, an imaginary part oscillates. The long-term fate of the whole system
is set by the eigenvalue with the largest real part, which is exactly the stability
criterion for a linear system.
</Statement>

## When there is no eigenbasis

Some matrices are defective: they do not have enough eigenvectors to diagonalise. The
exponential still exists, since the series never cared, but you compute it through the
Jordan form instead. A Jordan block splits as a multiple of the identity plus a nilpotent
part $N$, and a nilpotent's series terminates because $N^k = 0$ eventually:

$$
e^{Nt} = I + Nt + \frac{N^2 t^2}{2!} + \cdots + \frac{N^{k-1}t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}.
$$

<Aside>
Those finitely many extra terms are where factors of $t$, and $t^2$, in a solution come
from: a repeated eigenvalue with too few eigenvectors gives responses like $t\,e^{\lambda
t}$. It is the mathematical fingerprint of critical damping, the boundary between a system
that oscillates and one that just relaxes.
</Aside>

The payoff is a genuine unification. Coupled oscillators, radioactive decay chains, the
small-oscillation modes of the differential-equations notes, all of them are $\dot{\mathbf
x} = A\mathbf x$ for some $A$, and all of them are solved the same way: diagonalise, and
the coupled problem falls apart into scalar exponentials you already understand.
