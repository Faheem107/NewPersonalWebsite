Diagonalisation needs a square matrix and a cooperative one. The SVD needs nothing:

$$
A = U \Sigma V^\dagger
$$

for *any* $m \times n$ matrix, with $U$ and $V$ unitary and $\Sigma$ diagonal with
non-negative entries $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$.

## The geometry

Every linear map, read right to left, is: rotate ($V^\dagger$), scale along the axes
($\Sigma$), rotate again ($U$).

Equivalently: every matrix maps the unit sphere to an ellipsoid. The singular values
are the semi-axis lengths and the columns of $U$ are their directions. There are no
exceptions and no special cases.

<Plot src="notes/linear-algebra/svd.svg" n="1">
  The picture behind the algebra. $V$ picks which directions of the sphere to measure,
  $\Sigma$ stretches them by the singular values, and $U$ says where those stretched axes
  end up.
</Plot>

## Where it comes from

$A^\dagger A$ is Hermitian and positive semi-definite whatever $A$ was, so the spectral
theorem applies to it. Its eigenvectors are the columns of $V$, and
$\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$. The SVD is the spectral theorem laundered through a
matrix that is guaranteed to be well behaved.

## Low-rank approximation

<Theorem title="Eckart–Young">
Truncating the SVD after $k$ terms,

$$
A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \, u_i v_i^\dagger,
$$

gives the best rank-$k$ approximation to $A$ in both the spectral and Frobenius norms.
No other rank-$k$ matrix is closer.
</Theorem>

That optimality is what makes the SVD ubiquitous. Image compression keeps the large
$\sigma_i$ and discards the rest. Principal component analysis is the SVD of a centred
data matrix. Latent semantic analysis is the SVD of a term–document matrix. In each
case the singular values rank directions by how much variance they carry, and the tail
is usually noise.

## Conditioning

$$
\kappa(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}}
$$

The condition number bounds how much a relative error in the input can be amplified in
the output. A large $\kappa$ means the ellipsoid is a long thin sliver and the inverse
is dominated by whichever direction was squashed most.

<Statement kind="Practice" title="The pseudoinverse">
When $A$ is singular or rectangular, $A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^\dagger$, inverting the
non-zero singular values and transposing, gives the least-squares solution of minimum
norm. Truncating small $\sigma_i$ before inverting is regularisation, and it is the
standard fix for an ill-posed inverse problem such as deconvolving a microscope image.
</Statement>

## Why it beats eigendecomposition in practice

Eigenvectors of a non-symmetric matrix can be nearly parallel, which makes the change
of basis numerically hostile. The SVD's bases are orthonormal by construction, so the
decomposition is backward stable: the computed answer is the exact answer to a
slightly perturbed problem.

For anything involving measured data, that stability is worth more than the extra
structure diagonalisation would have given.
