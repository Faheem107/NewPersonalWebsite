The internal energy is a natural quantity to start from, and an awkward one to use. Its
differential

$$
dU = T\,dS - P\,dV
$$

tells you that $U$ is really a function of entropy and volume. Those are its natural
variables, the ones it wants to be written in. The trouble is that entropy is not
something you can set on a dial. Volume you can fix with a piston, temperature with a
bath, pressure with the weight of the atmosphere, but not entropy.

So the energy, in the variables it prefers, is written in terms of a quantity no
experiment controls directly. The thermodynamic potentials are the fix. Each one trades
an inconvenient variable for a convenient one, and the trade has a name.

## Trading a variable is a Legendre transform

The move is the same each time. To swap $S$ for its conjugate $T = (\partial U/\partial
S)_V$, subtract the product of the two:

$$
F = U - TS, \qquad dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV.
$$

The $T\,dS$ term has been cancelled and replaced by a $-S\,dT$, so $F$ is now a function
of $T$ and $V$. This is the Helmholtz free energy. Do the same to volume, or to both:

$$
H = U + PV, \qquad G = U - TS + PV.
$$

$H$ is the enthalpy, a function of $S$ and $P$; $G$ is the Gibbs free energy, a function
of $T$ and $P$, the two variables you can hold fixed most easily in a lab.

<Aside>
There is nothing thermodynamic about the Legendre transform itself. It is the same
operation that turns a Lagrangian into a Hamiltonian, swapping velocities for momenta.
Both times it exchanges a variable for the slope of the graph in that variable.
</Aside>

## Each potential is minimised under its own constraints

The second law says the entropy of the universe increases. Rewriting that in terms of a
system and a reservoir turns each potential into the thing that is extremised at
equilibrium under a particular set of held-fixed variables.

<Statement kind="Consequence" title="Which potential to watch">
At fixed $T$ and $V$, a system settles to the minimum of $F$. At fixed $T$ and $P$, it
settles to the minimum of $G$. The internal energy is minimised at fixed $S$ and $V$.
The rule is always the same: hold a potential's natural variables fixed, and that
potential is what the system minimises.
</Statement>

This is why $G$ does so much work in chemistry. Reactions on a bench happen at the
temperature of the room and the pressure of the air, so it is the Gibbs energy that
decides which way they run.

## Maxwell relations

Because each $dU$, $dF$, $dH$, $dG$ is an exact differential, its second mixed partials
are equal regardless of the order of differentiation. Applied to $dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV$,

$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V.
$$

The left side asks how entropy changes when you expand at fixed temperature, which is
hard to measure. The right side is just how pressure changes with temperature at fixed
volume, which is easy. The four potentials give four such identities, and their whole
value is this: they turn a quantity you cannot get at into one you can.

<Statement kind="Definition" title="A worked relation">
From $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$ the same equality of mixed partials gives $(\partial S/\partial
P)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P$. The right side is the thermal expansion coefficient,
tabulated for every material. The left side, the entropy released on compression, would
otherwise need a calorimeter.
</Statement>

None of this adds physics to the first and second laws. It is bookkeeping, and the
bookkeeping is the point: the potentials are four views of the same surface, chosen so
that whatever you happen to be able to hold fixed, one of them is written in exactly
those variables.
