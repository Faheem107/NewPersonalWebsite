title: The Four Laws
summary: Four statements discovered before anyone believed in atoms, which have survived every theory since.
order: 1
updated: May 2026
---

Put a hot cup of coffee on your desk. Walk away. Come back in an hour. The coffee is
cold, and you knew it would be.

Here is the strange part: every microscopic law governing the molecules in that cup is
symmetric in time. Run the equations backwards and they hold just as well. Nothing in
the microscopic physics prefers one direction. So why does the coffee always cool and
never spontaneously reheat?

Thermodynamics was built in the 1800s to answer questions like that, by people who had
no settled view on whether atoms existed. They found four principles that govern all
heat and energy processes, and numbered them zeroth through third — the zeroth was
discovered last but turned out to be the most fundamental.

## Zeroth: temperature exists

If $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with $C$, and $B$ is in equilibrium with $C$, then
$A$ is in equilibrium with $B$.

This sounds too obvious to be a law. But that transitivity is precisely what licenses
the idea of a temperature *scale* — a single number you can attach to a body and
compare against any other. Without it, thermometry would be meaningless.

## First: energy is conserved

$$
dU = \delta Q - \delta W
$$

The internal energy changes only through heat in or work out.

<Aside>
The notation is doing real work. $dU$ is an exact differential — $U$ is a property of
the state. $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ are not: heat and work are things that happen
*to* a system on the way between states, not things it possesses.
</Aside>

## Second: entropy never decreases

$$
\Delta S_{\text{universe}} \geq 0
$$

Clausius: heat never passes spontaneously from colder to warmer. Kelvin: no process
has as its sole result the conversion of heat entirely into work. The two statements
are equivalent, and both say entropy increases.

This is the one that gives time a direction. Everything else in physics runs equally
well backwards.

## Third: absolute zero is a limit, not a destination

As $T \to 0$, the entropy of a perfect crystal tends to zero. In statistical terms
$\Omega \to 1$ — a unique ground state.

The consequence is operational: each step of cooling removes a fraction of the
remaining energy, never a fixed amount, so reaching exactly zero requires infinitely
many steps. Absolute zero is unreachable in principle rather than merely difficult.

## The Carnot bound

Put the first and second together for a heat engine between reservoirs at $T_H$ and
$T_C$ and you get a ceiling on efficiency that no engineering can lift:

$$
\eta \leq 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}
$$

Nothing about the working fluid, the mechanism, or the century in which it was built
enters this. It is a statement about temperature ratios alone — which is why it was
correct before anyone knew what heat was.
