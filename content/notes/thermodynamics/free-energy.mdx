Water at one atmosphere boils at exactly 373.15 K. Not approximately — the coexistence
condition is sharp, and it comes from a single requirement.

## The condition

Two phases coexist when a molecule is indifferent about which one it is in. In
thermodynamic terms, the chemical potentials are equal:

$$
\mu_{\text{liquid}}(T, p) = \mu_{\text{gas}}(T, p)
$$

That is one equation in two unknowns, so its solutions form a curve in the $(T,p)$
plane — the phase boundary. Fix the pressure and the temperature is determined.

## Clausius–Clapeyron

Differentiate the coexistence condition along the boundary and the slope emerges:

$$
\frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{L}{T \, \Delta v}
$$

with $L$ the latent heat and $\Delta v$ the volume change per particle.

<Statement kind="Consequence" title="Why ice is strange">
For almost every substance $\Delta v > 0$ on melting and the boundary slopes forward:
squeeze it and it freezes. Water expands on freezing, so $\Delta v < 0$ and the
melting curve leans backwards — pressure *melts* ice. It is why a glacier slides on a
thin film of its own meltwater, and it is a direct consequence of the hydrogen bond
holding the crystal open.
</Statement>

## The critical point

Follow the liquid–gas boundary upward and the two phases become progressively more
alike — the liquid thins, the vapour densifies. At the critical point the distinction
disappears entirely and the boundary simply stops.

Above it there is no transition to cross. You can take a liquid to a gas continuously
by going around the critical point, never boiling, never seeing a meniscus.

<Aside>
Approaching $T_c$, density fluctuations grow until their correlation length reaches
the wavelength of visible light and the fluid scatters everything. It turns milky
white — critical opalescence — and it is the one place where you can watch a
diverging correlation length with your eyes.
</Aside>

## Order of a transition

Ehrenfest's classification is by which derivative of the free energy first jumps.

- **First order** — $S$ and $V$ are discontinuous. There is latent heat, the phases
  coexist, and you can watch a boundary between them. Boiling and melting.
- **Second order** — the first derivatives are continuous, the second ones (heat
  capacity, compressibility) diverge. No latent heat, no coexistence. The Curie point,
  the superfluid transition, the critical point itself.

Second-order transitions are where the universality of the previous section lives: at
a continuous transition the correlation length diverges, the system forgets its
microscopic structure, and utterly unrelated materials start behaving identically.
