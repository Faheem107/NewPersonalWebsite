Stand far enough from any lump of charge and you stop being able to see its details. A
complicated arrangement of positive and negative charge, viewed from across the room,
produces a field that depends on only a few numbers: the total charge, then how lopsided
it is, then how stretched. The multipole expansion is the statement of exactly which few
numbers, in order of how quickly they fade with distance.

## Expanding the distance

The potential of a distribution $\rho(\mathbf{r}')$ is an integral of $1/|\mathbf{r} -
\mathbf{r}'|$. For a field point far outside the charge, $r \gg r'$, that denominator
expands in Legendre polynomials of the angle $\gamma$ between $\mathbf{r}$ and
$\mathbf{r}'$:

$$
\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|} = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
\left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)^{\ell} P_\ell(\cos\gamma).
$$

Each power of $r'/r$ is smaller than the last, so the series is ordered by how fast each
term dies off with distance. Feeding it into the potential gives

$$
V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{Q}{r}
+ \frac{\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2}
+ \cdots\right].
$$

## The terms have names

The first term is the monopole: the total charge $Q$, its field falling as $1/r^2$ like a
point charge sitting at the origin. The second is the dipole,

$$
\mathbf{p} = \int \mathbf{r}'\,\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d^3r',
$$

with a field that falls faster, as $1/r^3$. Then the quadrupole at $1/r^4$, and so on. A
distribution with no net charge still has a dipole field; one with no dipole moment still
has a quadrupole. You only ever see the leading term that does not vanish, because it
dominates everything below it far away.

<Statement kind="Consequence" title="Why the leading term is origin-independent">
The dipole moment of a distribution generally depends on where you put the origin, since
shifting the origin by $\mathbf{a}$ changes $\mathbf{p}$ by $-Q\mathbf{a}$. But if the net
charge $Q$ is zero, that change vanishes and $\mathbf{p}$ is a genuine property of the
distribution. In general the first non-zero multipole moment is the one that does not
depend on the choice of origin, which is exactly why it is the one worth quoting.
</Statement>

## Why it is useful

The point of the expansion is not really the far field of a known charge. It is the
reverse: a neutral molecule, seen from outside, acts to leading order as a dipole, and
that single vector $\mathbf{p}$ captures how it interacts with fields and with other
molecules.

<Aside>
A water molecule carries a permanent dipole moment of about $6.2\times10^{-30}$ coulomb
metres. Nearly all of water's chemistry, from why it dissolves salt to why it beads on
wax, is that one number acting through the dipole field.
</Aside>

The energy of a dipole in an external field is $U = -\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}$, so it
wants to line up with the field, and the torque $\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{E}$ is what turns
it. This is the whole basis of how dielectrics respond and how a microwave oven heats
water: the field grabs the dipoles and the details of the molecule never enter.

So the multipole expansion is a way of forgetting, on purpose. It keeps precisely the few
numbers that survive being seen from far off, and it tells you the order in which to keep
them.
